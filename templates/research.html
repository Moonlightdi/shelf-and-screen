in my own words

What I learned

Before running the code, I wrote down my predictions. I was correct that horror has the darkest covers and is the easiest genre to identify, but most of the other results surprised me.

I thought colors would help guess the genre about 60% of the time, but the actual result was only 20%. This is still better than random guessing (12%), which means colors do provide some clues, but not many. Overall, you really can't judge a book by its cover, at least not based on its colors.

Science fiction was especially difficult to recognize, with only 1% accuracy. I think this is because sci-fi covers often use dark blues and blacks, similar to horror, causing the “judge” to confuse the two genres.

One of the biggest lessons was discovering a mistake in my own results. The data showed that book covers became darker since the 1850s, but this comparison was not accurate. Open Library provides the year a book was first written, while its cover usually belongs to a much newer edition. For example, a book written in 1850 might have a cover from 2015. Next time, I would use the publication year of each edition instead.

I also noticed that some genre labels were incorrect. For example, Open Library classified The Mayor of Casterbridge as horror. This reminded me that real-world data is not always reliable and that checking its accuracy is an important part of any project.

how I did it

Method

I took up to 150 books per genre from Open Library's subject lists and skipped books that appear in more than one genre. For each cover I found its five main colors with k-means clustering (palette.py) and measured brightness, colorfulness and the main hue. Then a simple "judge" (a nearest-centroid classifier) learned the average colors of each genre from 80% of the covers and tried to guess the genre of the other 20%, five times in a row with different splits (5-fold cross-validation). It never sees the title or the text, only colors. The code is in research.py, and the raw data in research_covers.csv.
